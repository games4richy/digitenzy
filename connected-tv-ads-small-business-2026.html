Connected TV Ads for Small Businesses in 2026: Real CPMs, Minimum Budgets, and How to Measure Results

By Marcus Reid8 min readPublished September 14, 2026

For most of advertising history, TV was out of reach for small businesses. You needed a production company, a media buyer, and a budget with several zeros. A local plumber or dental practice might afford a late-night slot on a regional channel, but targeting was crude and there was no real way to know whether anyone called because of it.

Streaming changed that. Connected TV, or CTV, lets businesses show video ads on apps like Roku channels, Tubi, Paramount+, Peacock, and others that people watch on their television. You can target by ZIP code, buy through self-serve dashboards, and start with budgets that would barely have covered a production meeting a decade ago. This guide covers what CTV really costs in 2026, where to buy it, how much you need to spend, and how to tell whether it's working.

What CTV Advertising Is (and Isn't)

CTV refers to video content streamed over the internet to a television screen, whether through a smart TV, a streaming stick, or a games console. CTV ads are the commercials that play during that content. They look like traditional TV ads, but they're bought and delivered digitally, which brings targeting and reporting closer to what you'd get from online ads.

It's worth separating CTV from a few related terms. OTT (over-the-top) is a broader label for streamed content on any device, including phones and laptops. YouTube on a TV screen counts as CTV viewing, and Google Ads can deliver video campaigns there. Linear TV is traditional broadcast and cable, bought by time slot and audience ratings.

Spending keeps shifting toward streaming. The IAB's 2026 outlook projects US digital video ad spend passing $80 billion, with CTV among the fastest-growing channels. More inventory has also helped push prices down, which is part of why the channel is now realistic for smaller advertisers.

These ranges come from 2026 market data published by AdWave and Paramount Ads Manager. CPM stands for cost per mille, the price of 1,000 ad impressions. A $26 CPM means roughly 2.6 cents each time your ad plays on a screen.

That sounds expensive next to social media, where CPMs can be a fraction of that. The comparison isn't quite fair, though. A CTV ad usually plays full screen, with sound on, in a living room, and viewers generally can't skip it. Completion rates are far higher than for most feed video. You're paying for attention that is much harder to get on a phone.

Prices climb as targeting narrows. Reaching everyone in a metro area is cheaper than reaching homeowners aged 35 to 54 with a household income above a certain level. Premium shows and live sports also cost more than older library content on free streaming apps.

Where Small Businesses Can Buy CTV Ads

A few years ago, most CTV buying ran through agencies and demand-side platforms with high minimums. Now there are several self-serve options built for smaller budgets:

  • Streaming publisher platforms. Roku, Disney, and Paramount all run self-serve ad managers, commonly with campaign minimums around $500. You buy directly against that publisher's inventory and audience data.
  • Free streaming services. Ad-supported apps such as Tubi tend to offer lower CPMs, which makes them a reasonable place to test with a small budget.
  • CTV-focused ad platforms. Tools like MNTN and tvScientific bundle inventory from many streaming apps and focus on performance measurement, linking TV exposure to website visits and conversions.
  • Amazon Ads. Amazon sells streaming TV ads, including on Prime Video, with the advantage of shopping-based audience data. Its self-serve options have opened up considerably, though larger buys still go through its DSP.
  • Google Ads. YouTube video campaigns can reach people watching on TV screens, and many small advertisers already have an account set up.

If you're new to the channel, platform comparisons can help narrow the list, but the practical choice usually comes down to three factors: the minimum spend, whether it can target your geography precisely, and what measurement it offers. For budgets under a few thousand dollars a month, a platform that aggregates inventory across many apps is usually simpler than buying from each publisher separately.

How Much Budget You Actually Need

The minimum a platform accepts isn't the same as the minimum that produces results. TV advertising works through repetition. One airing rarely changes behavior. Three or four exposures over a few weeks is closer to what it takes for someone to remember your name when they need you.

Here's the math for a local campaign. At a $30 CPM, $3,000 buys about 100,000 impressions. If you aim to show the ad around four times to each household, that reaches roughly 25,000 households in a month. In a mid-sized metro area, that might cover a meaningful slice of your target audience, or all of it if you narrow the geography to the ZIP codes you actually serve.

Work backwards from your numbers before you spend anything:

  1. Define your service area and roughly how many target households live there.
  2. Decide on frequency, usually three to five exposures per household per month.
  3. Multiply households by frequency to get the impressions you need.
  4. Multiply impressions by the CPM and divide by 1,000 to get your monthly budget.

If that number is far above what you can spend, shrink the geography or target a narrower audience rather than spreading a thin budget across a whole region. A focused campaign that people actually notice beats a broad one that nobody remembers. Plan to run for at least two to three months before judging results, because TV effects build over time.

Making a TV Ad Without a TV Budget

You don't need a cinematic commercial. You do need an ad that works on a big screen, watched from a sofa, often while someone is half paying attention. A few principles make a big difference:

  • Show your name and logo in the first few seconds and again at the end.
  • Say one thing. One offer or one reason to choose you. A list of every service you provide gets forgotten.
  • Invest in audio. Poor sound is more noticeable on a TV than poor video. A clear voiceover recorded in a quiet room goes a long way.
  • Use large, readable text. Phone-sized captions become unreadable at living room distance.
  • Make the next step easy to remember. A simple website address, a memorable phone number, or a QR code for viewers who have their phone nearby.

Fifteen and thirty seconds are the standard lengths. Shorter ads are cheaper to produce and easier to keep tight. Film real footage of your team and your work when you can, since it builds more trust than stock video. Our guide to ad creative strategy covers hooks and message clarity in more depth.

Measuring Results When Nobody Clicks

The hardest part of CTV for small businesses is proving it worked. Viewers can't click a TV ad, so you need other ways to connect exposure to action.

  • Website visit attribution. Many CTV platforms match households that saw your ad with later visits to your website from devices on the same network. Install the platform's tracking pixel before launching.
  • QR codes and vanity URLs. A dedicated landing page or code shown only in the TV ad gives you a direct signal, even if it undercounts.
  • Branded search lift. Watch whether searches for your business name rise in the areas where the ad runs, using Google Search Console and your ad account data.
  • Geographic comparison. Run the campaign in some ZIP codes but not similar nearby ones, then compare calls, leads, and sales between the two groups.
  • Ask customers. Add "Streaming TV ad" as an option when asking new customers how they found you.

No single method is perfect. Together they give a reasonable picture. The geographic comparison is the most reliable for local businesses, because it measures the overall effect on your business rather than depending on tracking that privacy settings can block.

Who Should Skip CTV (for Now)

CTV isn't the right first step for every business. If you haven't yet built solid search and social campaigns, those usually deliver faster and more measurable returns for the same money. A business that can't handle more calls or bookings won't benefit from more awareness. And if your whole marketing budget is a few hundred dollars a month, a TV campaign will be too thin to matter.

The businesses that tend to do well with CTV share a few traits: a clear local or regional market, a product or service worth hundreds or thousands of dollars per customer, and existing channels that can capture the extra demand TV creates. If that describes you, start with a tightly targeted test, set up measurement before launch, and give it enough time to build. For the first time, TV is a channel small businesses can try without betting the year's budget on it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a connected TV ad campaign cost?

Most CTV inventory is sold on a CPM basis, meaning a price per 1,000 impressions. In 2026 the average sits around $26, with most campaigns between $25 and $35 and premium, tightly targeted inventory costing more. Several self-serve platforms let small businesses start with around $500 per campaign, though a local campaign usually needs a few thousand dollars a month to reach enough households with enough frequency to see results.

Is CTV advertising worth it for a local business?

It can be, particularly for businesses with a meaningful average sale value and a clearly defined service area, such as home services, dental and medical practices, car dealers, gyms, and real estate. Businesses that depend on very low-cost impulse purchases or have only a few hundred dollars to spend usually get better returns from search and social ads first.

Do I need a professional TV commercial for CTV ads?

No. A clear, well-lit 15 or 30 second video with good audio, a simple message, and your brand shown early is enough for most small business campaigns. Some self-serve platforms include templates or tools to help create ads. What matters most is that the offer is easy to understand on a big screen from across a room.
