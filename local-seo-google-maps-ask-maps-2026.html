Local SEO in 2026: How to Rank in Google Maps and Ask Maps After Google's AI Overhaul

By Marcus Reid9 min readPublished September 14, 2026

Picture someone standing in their kitchen with a leaking water heater on a Sunday afternoon. A few years ago they would type "plumber near me" into Google and scroll through a map with three pins and a list of names. In 2026, a growing number of people open Google Maps and type the whole problem instead: "plumber open today who repairs tankless water heaters and has good reviews for showing up on time." Google no longer just returns a list. It gives an answer, often naming one or two businesses and explaining why.

That change comes from Ask Maps, the Gemini-powered layer Google added to Maps this year. For local businesses it moves the goal. Showing up in the top three pins still matters, but the bigger prize is being the business the AI picks when a customer describes exactly what they need. This guide covers how that selection works, what information feeds it, and the practical steps that make a local business easier to recommend.

What Ask Maps Changed About Local Search

Ask Maps launched in the US and India in March 2026 and rolled out to more than 150 additional countries in August. Instead of matching a short keyword against business names and categories, it reads a full question with conditions attached (opening hours, price level, parking, a specific service) and builds a response from Google's data on hundreds of millions of places.

The practical effect is that vague profiles lose ground. If a customer asks for a dentist who sees children and accepts a certain insurance plan, the AI can only recommend businesses whose profiles, reviews, or websites actually say so. A practice that treats kids but never mentions it anywhere online simply isn't part of the answer, even if it sits a block away.

Both trends reward businesses that have detailed, current information spread across the web.

How Google Still Decides Local Rankings

The AI layer is new, but the foundation underneath it is not. Google's own guidance on local ranking still lists three main factors, and Ask Maps leans on the same signals when it chooses whom to recommend.

  • Relevance: how well your business matches what the person is looking for. Categories, services, attributes, your description, review text, and website content all feed this.
  • Distance: how far you are from the searcher or the location they mention. You can't change your address, but you can make sure your service area is set correctly.
  • Prominence: how well known and trusted the business is. Review count and rating, mentions on other sites, links to your website, and your organic search strength all play a part.

What has changed is how finely relevance gets judged. A keyword search for "Italian restaurant" treats every Italian restaurant roughly the same. A question about "a quiet Italian place with vegan pasta that takes reservations for eight" separates businesses on details most owners never bothered to list. Local SEO in 2026 is largely the work of getting those details written down in places Google can read.

Building a Business Profile the AI Can Actually Use

Your Google Business Profile is still the local asset that carries the most weight. Most local profiles are technically complete but thin: a name, a phone number, hours, and a handful of photos from when the business opened. That was enough when rankings were mostly about distance and review count. It isn't enough when the AI needs to answer specific questions.

Get the categories right

Your primary category carries the most weight, so choose the one that describes your core business as precisely as possible. "Family law attorney" is more useful than "lawyer." Add secondary categories only for services that make up a meaningful part of the business. Adding loosely related categories to widen your reach tends to blur relevance rather than help it.

Fill in services, products, and attributes

The services list, product entries, and attributes (wheelchair accessible, free Wi-Fi, women-owned, appointment required, and so on) are exactly the kind of structured facts Ask Maps uses to filter options. Go through every attribute Google offers for your category and answer each one honestly. Write short service descriptions in plain language that match how customers describe the job, not internal jargon.

Keep the name clean

Use your real-world business name, the one on your sign and your invoices. Adding keywords or locations to the name ("Smith Plumbing Best Emergency Plumber Austin") breaks Google's Business Profile guidelines. Local SEO forums have reported a wave of suspensions for this kind of keyword stuffing in 2026, and getting a suspended profile reinstated can take weeks of lost calls.

Show that the business is active

Post updates, add new photos, and keep holiday hours current. There is no official rule that inactive profiles get demoted, but recent activity is a sign that the information is trustworthy, and outdated hours are one of the fastest ways to earn a bad review. Real photos of your team, premises, and finished work also give the AI and the customer something concrete to go on.

Quick profile audit: Ask yourself the five questions customers most often call you about (parking, pricing, payment options, whether you handle a specific job, how soon you can come out). If the answers aren't visible on your profile or website, add them this week. Those are the questions people now type straight into Maps.

Reviews Now Do Double Duty

Reviews have always influenced rankings and trust. In an AI-driven Maps experience they also act as a source of facts. When a customer writes that your team "came out within two hours and fixed our tankless heater," that sentence tells Google something no category can: you handle tankless heaters, and you respond quickly. The text of reviews matters almost as much as the star rating.

You can't script reviews, and you shouldn't try. What you can do is make it easy for satisfied customers to leave one and gently prompt them to be specific. A short follow-up message with a direct review link, sent the day the job is finished, works far better than a generic request weeks later. Asking "would you mind mentioning what we helped with?" is fair. Offering discounts in exchange for reviews, or only asking customers you expect to be happy, is not.

Be careful here. The US Federal Trade Commission's rule on fake reviews and testimonials allows civil penalties for buying reviews, writing fake ones, or suppressing negative ones. Google also removes reviews it detects as incentivized and can restrict profiles that collect them.

Reply to reviews, including the critical ones. A calm, specific response to a complaint shows future customers how you handle problems, and BrightLocal's research consistently finds that people expect businesses to respond. Keep replies human and brief. Copy-pasting the same "Thanks for your feedback!" under every review adds nothing.

Your Website Still Feeds the Answer

Ask Maps pulls from more than your profile. Your website fills in the details a profile can't hold, and it supports the prominence side of ranking through organic search strength. A few pages do most of the work.

  • One page per core service. A page titled "Tankless Water Heater Repair in Austin" that explains what you fix, typical timelines, and rough price ranges answers far more questions than a single "Services" page with a bulleted list.
  • Location or service-area pages that say something real. Mention the neighborhoods you cover, local projects, and any area-specific details. Pages that swap the city name into otherwise identical text don't help.
  • An FAQ written from real calls. Use the questions customers actually ask your front desk.
  • Consistent name, address, and phone number. Match your profile exactly, and keep directory listings consistent with it.

Adding LocalBusiness structured data to your homepage or contact page helps search engines connect the website to the business entity, including hours, address, and the area you serve. It won't lift rankings on its own, but it removes ambiguity, which is exactly what AI systems struggle with.

Local Visibility Beyond Google Maps

Google remains the biggest source of local discovery, but it is no longer the only one people rely on. BrightLocal's 2026 data showed Google's share of review usage falling from 83% to 71% while AI assistants climbed sharply. Those assistants build their answers from sources across the web, so a business that only exists on Google is easier to overlook.

Claim and complete your listings on Apple Business Connect, which controls how you appear in Apple Maps and Siri, and on Bing Places, which feeds Microsoft's search products. Keep Yelp and the major directories in your industry accurate too. The goal isn't to chase every listing site. It's to make sure the handful that AI tools read all tell the same, detailed story about your business.

How to Measure Local Performance Now

Ranking trackers that check a single keyword from one location give an increasingly partial picture, because Ask Maps answers depend on the whole question. A better setup combines a few signals.

  • Business Profile performance data: calls, direction requests, website clicks, and bookings from the profile, tracked month over month.
  • Tagged website links: add UTM parameters to the website link on your profile so profile visits show up separately in Google Analytics.
  • Manual question tests: once a month, ask Maps and a couple of AI assistants the ten questions your customers most often ask, and note whether your business appears. Test from different parts of your service area.
  • Call and lead tracking: ask new customers how they found you and log the answer. "Google Maps" and "ChatGPT recommended you" are now both common replies.

Local SEO has always rewarded businesses that are good at what they do and easy to understand online. The AI overhaul makes that second part more demanding. The businesses that keep winning in 2026 are the ones that write down the specifics, earn detailed reviews honestly, and keep every listing current, so that when a customer asks a very particular question, the answer points to them.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Ask Maps in Google Maps?

Ask Maps is a Gemini-powered feature inside Google Maps that lets people ask full questions, such as which nearby bakery has gluten-free options and is open late, and get a direct recommendation instead of a plain list. It launched in the US and India in March 2026 and expanded to more than 150 more countries in August 2026. Its answers draw on Business Profile details, reviews, photos, and information from business websites.

Can I pay to rank higher in Ask Maps?

No. Organic local rankings and Ask Maps recommendations can't be bought. Google Local Services Ads and Maps ads are separate paid placements that are labeled as sponsored. The organic answer depends on how relevant, close, and well regarded your business is, which comes down to profile accuracy, review quality, and the information on your website.

How many reviews does a local business need?

There is no fixed number, because Google compares you with nearby competitors. What matters more in 2026 is a steady flow of recent reviews that describe specific services, plus replies from the owner. BrightLocal's 2026 survey found that 31% of consumers will only use businesses rated 4.5 stars or higher, so rating and recency both count.
